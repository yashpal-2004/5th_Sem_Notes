Why the Vanilla RNN Forgets

1.1 Vanilla RNN Formulation & Continuous Overwriting

In standard Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), processing a sequence of tokens $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_T$ relies on a single hidden state vector $h_t$ that serves simultaneously as the working memory and the network output:

$$h_t = \tanh\left( w \cdot x_t + w_h \cdot h_{t-1} + b \right)$$

Where:

  • $x_t$: Input token embedding at timestep $t$.
  • $w$: Input-to-hidden projection weight.
  • $w_h$: Recurrent hidden-to-hidden weight.
  • $b$: Bias parameter.
  • $\tanh$: Hyperbolic tangent non-linearity squeezing values strictly into $(-1, 1)$.
The Fundamental Flaw: Loss of Separation

At every incoming word, the RNN forces the network to calculate a weighted sum and squeeze it through $\tanh$. Because there is no persistent storage separate from the active state, every new word overwrites part of the past memory.

1.2 Hand Calculation: Step-by-Step Sentiment Decay

Consider the task of sentiment classification across two benchmark movie reviews:

  • Review A (Target = Positive): "The movie was brilliant, and I watched it with my friends on a Sunday evening." (16 words total; sentiment word appears at word 4, followed by 12 neutral words).
  • Review B (Target = Positive): "The movie was long and slow, but the ending was brilliant." (Sentiment flips at "but"; initial negative memory must be discarded).

Let each word be represented as a 4-dimensional feature vector: $[\text{positive}, \text{negative}, \text{turn}, \text{end}]^T$:

  • "brilliant": $[1, 0, 0, 0]^T$
  • "long": $[0, 0.5, 0, 0]^T$
  • "slow": $[0, 1, 0, 0]^T$
  • "but": $[0, 0, 1, 0]^T$
  • "." (verdict): $[0, 0, 0, 1]^T$
  • Neutral words ("the", "movie", "was", "and", "I"): $[0, 0, 0, 0]^T$

Given parameters: $w^{\text{pos}} = 2.0$, $w^{\text{neg}} = -2.0$, $w_h = 0.5$, and $b = 0$:

  1. Step 1 to 3 (Neutral words): $w \cdot x_t = 0 \implies h_1 = h_2 = h_3 = 0$.
  2. Step 4 ("brilliant"): $w \cdot x_4 = 2 \times 1 = 2 \implies h_4 = \tanh(2 + 0.5 \times 0) = \tanh(2) \approx 0.96$.
  3. Step 5 ("and"): $w \cdot x_5 = 0 \implies h_5 = \tanh(0 + 0.5 \times 0.96) = \tanh(0.48) \approx 0.45$.
  4. Step 6 ("I"): $w \cdot x_6 = 0 \implies h_6 = \tanh(0 + 0.5 \times 0.45) = \tanh(0.22) \approx 0.22$.
Mathematical Insight: The Small-Angle Tanh Approximation

For small values of $z$, $\tanh(z) \approx z$. Since $w \cdot x_t = 0$ for neutral words: $$h_t = \tanh(w_h \cdot h_{t-1}) \approx w_h \cdot h_{t-1} = 0.5 \cdot h_{t-1}$$ Every neutral word halves the remaining memory signal! After 12 neutral words: $$h_{16} \approx (0.5)^{12} \times 0.96 \approx 0.00023$$ By the final punctuation mark, the evidence that the movie was "brilliant" has decayed to zero.

1.3 Vanishing Gradients Across Long Token Gaps

During training via Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT), the gradient of the loss with respect to $h_4$ must flow backward through 12 Jacobians:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_4} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_{16}} \prod_{k=5}^{16} \frac{\partial h_k}{\partial h_{k-1}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_{16}} \prod_{k=5}^{16} \left( w_h \cdot (1 - \tanh^2(z_k)) \right)$$

Since $\tanh'(z) \le 1.0$ and $w_h < 1$, the gradient diminishes exponentially ($\approx 0.018\%$). The network cannot compute a meaningful parameter update for early tokens, making vanilla RNNs fundamentally incapable of bridging long-range contextual gaps.

2

The Additive Long-Term Memory Lane ($C_t$)

2.1 The Two-Memory Separation ($C_t$ vs. $h_t$)

To eliminate memory degradation, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) decouples working memory into two concurrent streams:

  • Long-Term Memory ($C_t$, Cell State): A dedicated vector that persists across dozens or hundreds of timesteps. Modifications occur primarily via pure addition rather than destructive matrix multiplication.
  • Short-Term Memory ($h_t$, Hidden State): The immediate output extracted from $C_t$ at timestep $t$, passed to the next recurrent step and the task classifier.

2.2 Candidate Generation ($\tilde{C}_t$) via Tanh

At each timestep, the network analyzes the current word $x_t$ and previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$ to propose a candidate update $\tilde{C}_t$:

$$\tilde{C}_t = \tanh\left( w_C \cdot x_t + w_{hC} \cdot h_{t-1} + b_C \right)$$

If the network simply adds the candidate update directly: $$C_t = C_{t-1} + \tilde{C}_t$$ $$h_t = \tanh(C_t)$$

Under Review A:

  • Neutral words produce $\tilde{C}_t = 0 \implies C_t = C_{t-1} + 0 = C_{t-1}$.
  • "brilliant" adds $+1.00 \implies C_4 = 1.00$.
  • Across the subsequent 12 neutral words, adding $0$ leaves $C_t = 1.00$ completely intact!
  • At the verdict ($t=16$): $h_{16} = \tanh(1.00) = 0.76 > 0$ → Positive prediction succeeds!

2.3 Failure of Pure Addition: The Inability to Forget

While naive addition solves Review A, it completely fails on Review B ("The movie was long and slow, but the ending was brilliant."):

  1. "long" ($x_4$): Candidate $\tilde{C}_4 = -0.96 \implies C_4 = -0.96$.
  2. "slow" ($x_6$): Candidate $\tilde{C}_6 = -1.00 \implies C_6 = -0.96 + (-1.00) = -1.96$.
  3. "but" ($x_7$): Turn word adds $0 \implies C_7 = -1.96$.
  4. "brilliant" ($x_{11}$): Candidate adds $+1.00 \implies C_{11} = -1.96 + 1.00 = -0.96$.
  5. Verdict ($t=12$): $h_{12} = \tanh(-0.96) = -0.75$ → Incorrect Negative Verdict!
The Need for Gating

One positive word cannot cancel out two negative words under unweighted addition! A network must not only add memories—it must possess a trainable mechanism to erase outdated information when a sentiment flip occurs.

3

Gating Mechanism: Multiplicative Switches

3.1 Anatomy of a Gate: Affine Score Squeezed by Sigmoid ($\sigma$)

A gate in deep learning is a learned continuous filter ranging strictly between $0$ and $1$. It acts as a multiplicative valve on vector elements:

$$\text{Gate} = \sigma\left( \sum \right) = \sigma\left( w_{\text{gate}} \cdot x_t + w_{h,\text{gate}} \cdot h_{t-1} + b_{\text{gate}} \right)$$

Where the standard sigmoid activation function is defined as:

$$\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$$
Gate Output ($\sigma(z)$) Physical Interpretation Multiplication Effect on Memory
$1.0$ (Open) Score $z \gg 0$ (e.g., $\sigma(9) \approx 1.0$) $(-1.96) \times 1.0 = -1.96$ → Keep 100% of information
$0.5$ (Half) Score $z = 0$ (e.g., $\sigma(0) = 0.5$) $(-1.96) \times 0.5 = -0.98$ → Keep 50% of information
$0.0$ (Closed) Score $z \ll 0$ (e.g., $\sigma(-9) \approx 0.0$) $(-1.96) \times 0.0 = 0.00$ → Completely erase / forget

3.2 The Forget Gate ($f_t$): Learning When to Erase

The Forget Gate controls what fraction of the previous cell state $C_{t-1}$ is preserved:

$$f_t = \sigma\left( w_f \cdot x_t + w_{hf} \cdot h_{t-1} + b_f \right)$$

In our sentiment model, training discovers:

  • On neutral words ($x_t = \text{neutral}$): $\Sigma_f = b_f = +9 \implies f_t = \sigma(9) \approx 1.0$ (Preserve memory).
  • On the contrastive conjunction "but": Feature vector activates $w_f^{\text{turn}} = -18$ with $b_f = 9$: $$\Sigma_f = (-18 \times 1) + 9 = -9 \implies f_t = \sigma(-9) \approx 0.0$$

Multiplying $C_{t-1}$ by $f_t = 0$ completely resets the negative sentiment from "long and slow" to exactly $0.00$, preparing a clean slate for "brilliant".

3.3 The Three Gates at a Glance: $f_t$, $i_t$, and $o_t$

Gate Symbol Name Primary Role Operational Behavior
$f_t$ Forget Gate $\%$ of old cell state $C_{t-1}$ to retain $1$ on neutral and sentiment words; drops to $0$ on turn words like "but".
$i_t$ Input Gate $\%$ of candidate $\tilde{C}_t$ to write into $C_t$ $1$ on emotionally charged words ("brilliant", "slow"); $0$ on neutral filler words.
$o_t$ Output Gate $\%$ of cell state $C_t$ to reveal to $h_t$ $0$ throughout the sentence; opens to $1$ only at the sentence boundary punctuation ".".

The Complete LSTM Architecture

4.1 Full Vector Equations & Forward Equations

At every step $t$, the standard LSTM cell executes four affine transformations followed by cell state and hidden state updates:

The Complete LSTM Equations
$$\begin{aligned} f_t &= \sigma\left( W_f x_t + U_f h_{t-1} + b_f \right) && \text{[Forget Gate: What to erase from } C_{t-1} \text{]} \\ i_t &= \sigma\left( W_i x_t + U_i h_{t-1} + b_i \right) && \text{[Input Gate: What to write to } C_t \text{]} \\ \tilde{C}_t \equiv g_t &= \tanh\left( W_c x_t + U_c h_{t-1} + b_c \right) && \text{[Candidate State: New proposed content]} \\ C_t &= f_t \odot C_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{C}_t && \text{[Cell State Update: Additive Highway]} \\ o_t &= \sigma\left( W_o x_t + U_o h_{t-1} + b_o \right) && \text{[Output Gate: What to reveal]} \\ h_t &= o_t \odot \tanh(C_t) && \text{[Hidden State: Filtered readout]} \end{aligned}$$

Notation Note: In modern machine learning frameworks such as PyTorch, the candidate state $\tilde{C}_t$ is canonically designated as $g_t$: $$g_t = \tanh\left( W_{ig} x_t + W_{hg} h_{t-1} + b_g \right)$$

All gate operations ($f_t \odot C_{t-1}$, $i_t \odot \tilde{C}_t$, and $o_t \odot \tanh(C_t)$) employ the element-wise Hadamard product ($\odot$).

PyTorch Implementation Deep Dive: Weight Unpacking Order

In PyTorch's native nn.LSTM module, for maximum GPU kernel efficiency via fused GEMM (General Matrix Multiply), the four gate parameter matrices are concatenated into a single master tensor weight_ih ($4h \times d$) and weight_hh ($4h \times h$): 

# PyTorch internal gate arrangement order: IFGO
# Input (i), Forget (f), Cell/Candidate (g), Output (o)

# Splitting weight matrices into the 4 constituent gates:
W_ii, W_if, W_ig, W_io = rnn.weight_ih_l0.chunk(4, dim=0)
W_hi, W_hf, W_hg, W_ho = rnn.weight_hh_l0.chunk(4, dim=0)
b_i,  b_f,  b_g,  b_o  = (rnn.bias_ih_l0 + rnn.bias_hh_l0).chunk(4, dim=0)

Critical Takeaway: When decomposing PyTorch LSTM parameters via .chunk(4, dim=0), always assign them in the strict order: Input ($i$), Forget ($f$), Candidate/Cell ($g$), Output ($o$).

4.2 Tracing Review B Word-by-Word: Overwriting via Gating

Let us observe how the full gated mechanism processes Review B:

Token ($t$) $f_t$ $i_t$ $\tilde{C}_t$ $C_t = f_t C_{t-1} + i_t \tilde{C}_t$ $o_t$ $h_t = o_t \tanh(C_t)$
"The" $1.00$ $0.00$ $0.00$ $1.00(0.00) + 0(0) = \mathbf{0.00}$ $0.00$ $\mathbf{0.00}$
"long" $1.00$ $0.50$ $-0.96$ $1.00(0.00) + 0.50(-0.96) = \mathbf{-0.48}$ $0.00$ $\mathbf{0.00}$
"slow" $1.00$ $1.00$ $-1.00$ $1.00(-0.48) + 1.00(-1.00) = \mathbf{-1.48}$ $0.00$ $\mathbf{0.00}$
"but" $\mathbf{0.00}$ $0.00$ $0.00$ $\mathbf{0.00}(-1.48) + 0(0) = \mathbf{0.00}$ $0.00$ $\mathbf{0.00}$
"brilliant" $1.00$ $1.00$ $+1.00$ $1.00(0.00) + 1.00(1.00) = \mathbf{1.00}$ $0.00$ $\mathbf{0.00}$
"." (verdict) $1.00$ $0.00$ $0.00$ $1.00(1.00) + 0(0) = \mathbf{1.00}$ $\mathbf{1.00}$ $1.00 \times \tanh(1.00) = \mathbf{+0.76}$
Why the Verdict Succeeds

At "but", the forget gate completely zeroes out the $-1.48$ negative penalty. When "brilliant" arrives, it writes $+1.00$ onto an empty slate. The output gate stays closed ($0.00$) throughout the text, preventing intermediate tokens from polluting $h_t$, and opens exclusively at the terminal period to expose the classification decision.

4.3 Gradient Highway: Unbroken Backpropagation over Time

In backpropagation through time for an LSTM, the gradient flows directly along the cell state channel:

$$\frac{\partial C_t}{\partial C_{t-1}} = f_t$$

If the network learns to keep $f_t \approx 1.00$ across neutral tokens, the gradient Jacobian product is:

$$\frac{\partial C_T}{\partial C_0} = \prod_{k=1}^T f_k \approx 1.00 \times 1.00 \times \dots = 1.00$$

This forms a literal Constant Error Carousel (CEC) or gradient highway, completely shielding the gradient from vanishing regardless of how many words separate the cause from the effect!

The Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)

5.1 Design Philosophy: Streamlining Memory into One State

Introduced by Cho et al. (2014), the Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) compresses the LSTM architecture into a lighter, computationally faster formulation by combining the cell state $C_t$ and hidden state $h_t$ into a single hidden vector $h_t$.

5.2 Coupled Update Gate ($z_t$) and Reset Gate ($r_t$)

The GRU replaces the separate forget and input gates with a single coupled update slider $z_t$:

$$\begin{aligned} z_t &= \sigma\left( W_z x_t + U_z h_{t-1} + b_z \right) && \text{[Update Gate: Slider between past & candidate]} \\ r_t &= \sigma\left( W_r x_t + U_r h_{t-1} + b_r \right) && \text{[Reset Gate: How much of past to expose to candidate]} \\ \tilde{h}_t &= \tanh\left( W_h x_t + U_h (r_t \odot h_{t-1}) + b_h \right) && \text{[Candidate Hidden State]} \\ h_t &= (1 - z_t) \odot h_{t-1} + z_t \odot \tilde{h}_t && \text{[Linear Interpolation Update]} \end{aligned}$$
The Update Gate as a Conservation Slider

The fraction of the old memory kept is $(1 - z_t)$, while the fraction of the new candidate written is $z_t$. Because: $$(1 - z_t) + z_t = 1.0$$ The two shares always sum strictly to 1. The network can never accidentally duplicate or multiply memory intensity.

The Role of the Reset Gate ($r_t$):
At the turn word "but", even if $z_t = 1.0$ (commanding 100% write of $\tilde{h}_t$), the candidate formula would still read $h_{t-1} = -0.76$ if uncontrolled! The reset gate drops to $r_t = 0.0$, applying $r_t \odot h_{t-1} = 0.0$ and ensuring that candidate $\tilde{h}_t$ starts completely fresh without old negativity.

5.3 Comparative Matrix: LSTM vs. GRU

Architectural Feature Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)
Memory Representation Two states: Cell State $C_t$ & Hidden State $h_t$ Single unified hidden state $h_t$
Gate Inventory 3 gates ($f_t$ forget, $i_t$ input, $o_t$ output) 2 gates ($z_t$ update, $r_t$ reset)
Erase & Write Mechanism Independent gates ($f_t$ and $i_t$) Coupled slider: keep $(1 - z_t)$, write $z_t$
Parameter Count ($\Sigma$ boxes) 4 affine projections: $f_t, i_t, \tilde{C}_t \equiv g_t, o_t$ 3 affine projections: $z_t, r_t, \tilde{h}_t$
Computational Speed Slower; higher GPU memory footprint $\sim 25\%$ faster execution; fewer parameters to train
Long-Gap Protection Yes, via additive channel: $f_t \odot C_{t-1}$ Yes, via slider channel: $(1 - z_t) \odot h_{t-1}$
6

Bidirectional RNNs (BiRNN) & Self-Check Review

6.1 Future Context Ambiguity: Forward vs. Backward Readers

Standard unidirectional RNNs read text strictly left-to-right. However, in human language, the true semantic meaning of a word frequently depends on words that appear after it:

  • Sentence 1: "He said Teddy bears are on sale."
  • Sentence 2: "He said Teddy Roosevelt was a president."

When a forward reader reaches "Teddy", it has only seen "He said", making it impossible to distinguish between a plush toy and a historical US president!

6.2 Concatenated Hidden States & Generative Limitations

A Bidirectional Recurrent Neural Network (BiRNN) resolves this ambiguity by deploying two independent recurrent layers running in opposite directions over the same sequence:

  1. Forward Layer ($\overrightarrow{h}_t$): Reads sequence from left to right: $x_1 \rightarrow x_2 \rightarrow \dots \rightarrow x_T$.
  2. Backward Layer ($\overleftarrow{h}_t$): Reads sequence from right to left: $x_T \rightarrow x_{T-1} \rightarrow \dots \rightarrow x_1$.

The final contextual representation for token $t$ concatenates both states:

$$h_t = \left[ \overrightarrow{h}_t \;;\; \overleftarrow{h}_t \right]$$

At the token "Teddy", the backward reader has already ingested "bears are on sale" in Sentence 1 and "Roosevelt was a president" in Sentence 2, allowing unambiguous feature representation.

Critical Generative Limitation

Why can a Bidirectional RNN NOT generate text word-by-word?
Autoregressive generation (such as GPT text completion) predicts word $t+1$ given past tokens. A backward reader requires the complete sentence to exist before it can execute. Because future tokens are not yet written, backward passes cannot execute at generation time.

6.3 Worksheet Exit Ticket Analysis

Worksheet Exit Ticket Review & Rationales

Q1: Which line represents the LSTM memory update?
Correct: B) $C_t = f_t \cdot C_{t-1} + i_t \cdot \tilde{C}_t$
Rationale: The cell state updates additively using the forget gate $f_t$ and input gate $i_t$.

Q2: Why does the LSTM keep "brilliant" until the end of Review A?
Correct: C) The forget gate keeps it ($f_t \approx 1.00$), and small words write nothing because $i_t \approx 0$.
Rationale: Neutral tokens produce $i_t = 0$, so adding $0$ leaves $C_t$ completely invariant across the 12-word gap.

Q3: What does a GRU have?
Correct: C) One memory $h_t$ and two gates, $z_t$ and $r_t$.
Rationale: GRU combines cell and hidden states into a single vector $h_t$ and utilizes only update and reset gates.

Q4: In a GRU with $z_t = 0.9$, how much of $h_{t-1}$ is kept?
Correct: B) $0.1$
Rationale: The retention share is $(1 - z_t) = 1 - 0.9 = 0.1$.
