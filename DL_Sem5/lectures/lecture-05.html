Executive Summary & Formula Sheet

Core Concept: Gradient descent updates parameters by moving opposite to the gradient: \(w_{t+1} = w_t - \alpha \nabla L(w_t)\). The choice of batch size balances gradient precision against computation speed. Selecting and scheduling learning rate \(\alpha\) is critical: rates that are too high cause divergence, while rates that are too low stall convergence.

Formula Sheet & Key Rules
  • Gradient Descent Update: \(w_{t+1} = w_t - \alpha \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_t}\)
  • Batch GD: Uses all \(N\) samples per step (exact, slow).
  • Stochastic GD (SGD): Uses 1 sample per step (noisy, fast).
  • Mini-batch GD: Uses batch size \(B \in [32, 256]\) (balanced GPU parallelization).
  • Step Decay: \(\alpha_t = \alpha_0 \cdot \gamma^{\lfloor t / S \rfloor}\)
  • Cosine Annealing: \(\alpha_t = \alpha_{\min} + \frac{1}{2}(\alpha_{\max} - \alpha_{\min})\left(1 + \cos\left(\frac{t}{T_{\max}}\pi\right)\right)\)

1. Intuition of Downhill Steps

1.1 Foggy Hill Analogy & Local Slope

Imagine walking down a foggy mountain blindfolded. You feel the slope directly under your feet. Taking small steps in the steepest downhill direction eventually leads to the valley floor (loss minimum).

1.2 Mathematical Loss Function Minimization

The gradient \(\nabla L(w)\) points in the direction of steepest loss increase:

$$w_{t+1} = w_t - \alpha \nabla L(w_t)$$

2

2. Batch GD vs. SGD vs. Mini-batch GD

2.1 Full-Batch Gradient Descent

Calculates average gradient across all \(N\) dataset samples. Guaranteed smooth convergence to minimum for convex loss, but requires massive memory and compute per single parameter step.

2.2 Stochastic & Mini-Batch Trade-offs

SGD: Updates weights using 1 random sample. High variance causes noisy trajectories, which helps escape shallow local minima but prevents exact convergence without learning rate decay.

Mini-batch GD: Uses mini-batches of size \(B\) (e.g., 32 or 64). Combines vectorization efficiency of GPUs with regularizing noise of SGD.

3

3. Learning Rate Pathology & Diagnosis

3.1 Under-stepping, Oscillation & Divergence

Too Small (\(\alpha = 0.0001\)): Loss decreases extremely slowly; model gets stuck in plateaus.

Optimal (\(\alpha = 0.01\)): Smooth logarithmic decline to minimum.

Too High (\(\alpha = 0.5\)): Loss oscillates back and forth across valley walls without descending.

Very High (\(\alpha = 2.0\)): Steps overshoot the opposite slope, loss explodes to \(\infty\) or NaN (divergence).

3.2 Loss Trajectory Visual Signatures

Monitoring training loss curves over epochs reveals learning rate health: linear slow drop indicates low \(\alpha\), explosive vertical spikes indicate high \(\alpha\), and steady exponential decay signals well-tuned \(\alpha\).

4

4. Learning-Rate Schedules

4.1 Step Decay & Exponential Decay

Starting with a larger \(\alpha\) allows rapid initial progress. Periodically reducing \(\alpha\) (e.g., halving every 10 epochs) lets the model settle into precise minima late in training.

4.2 Cosine Annealing Schedule

Cosine Annealing Formula:

$$\alpha_t = \alpha_{\min} + \frac{1}{2}(\alpha_{\max} - \alpha_{\min})\left(1 + \cos\left(\frac{t}{T_{\max}}\pi\right)\right)$$
5

5. Learning Rate Warm-up

5.1 Why Early High Rates Destabilize Training

At initialization, randomly set weights produce large unstable gradients. Applying high learning rates immediately can permanently distort early feature representations.

5.2 Linear Warmup Formulation

Warm-up linearly ramps \(\alpha\) from 0 to \(\alpha_{\text{base}}\) over the first few epochs (e.g., 5 epochs), giving initial gradients time to stabilize before high-speed optimization begins.

6. Worked Gradient Descent Calculation

6.1 Single Variable Loss \(L(w) = (w-3)^2\)

Gradient \(\frac{dL}{dw} = 2(w-3)\). Target minimum is at \(w^* = 3\).

6.2 Multi-step Convergence Tracing

Starting from \(w_0 = 0\) with learning rate \(\alpha = 0.1\):

  • Step 1: \(\nabla L(0) = 2(0-3) = -6 \implies w_1 = 0 - 0.1(-6) = 0.6\)
  • Step 2: \(\nabla L(0.6) = 2(0.6-3) = -4.8 \implies w_2 = 0.6 - 0.1(-4.8) = 1.08\)
  • Step 3: \(\nabla L(1.08) = 2(1.08-3) = -3.84 \implies w_3 = 1.08 - 0.1(-3.84) = 1.464\)
  • Notice \(w_t\) smoothly approaches 3 at rate \((1 - 2\alpha)^t = 0.8^t\).
