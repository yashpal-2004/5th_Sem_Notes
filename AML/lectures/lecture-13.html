Core principles and summary cheat-sheet for Lecture 13:

Time Series Fundamentals & Core Takeaways
  • Violation of I.I.D.: In cross-sectional data, observations are independent and row order is arbitrary. In time series, temporal ordering carries the essential signal; shuffling rows destroys the data structure. Standard K-fold cross-validation is invalid and causes severe temporal data leakage.
  • Decomposition: An observed series \(Y_t\) is decomposed into Trend (long-term trajectory), Seasonality (fixed, repeating cyclic patterns), and Noise / Shocks (\(\varepsilon_t\), unpredictable residuals). A forecasting model is successful when its residuals behave as pure white noise.
  • Stationarity Requirement: Classical models require constant mean, constant variance, and autocovariance that depends only on lag displacement \(k\), not absolute time \(t\).
  • Lag Differencing: \(\Delta Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-1}\) eliminates linear trends by modeling step-to-step changes rather than raw absolute values.
  • Auto Regression AR(\(p\)): Predicts \(y_t\) as a linear combination of its own \(p\) historical lags: \(y_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \dots + \phi_p y_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t\) (models momentum). Order \(p\) is selected via significant spikes on the PACF plot.
  • Moving Average MA(\(q\)): Predicts \(y_t\) as a linear combination of \(q\) past random shocks (forecast errors): \(y_t = c + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1 \varepsilon_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q \varepsilon_{t-q}\) (models shock absorption, NOT a rolling mean). Order \(q\) is selected via the ACF plot.
01

Why Time Matters & Time Series Decomposition

1.1 Violation of the I.I.D. Assumption

In classical machine learning (e.g., linear regression, classification on tabular datasets), we assume that observations are Independent and Identically Distributed (I.I.D.). If you shuffle 500 house price listings before training, the learned regression weights remain completely unchanged because row 1 (a house in Delhi) has no dependency on row 2 (a house in Mumbai).

In a Time Series, observations are explicitly dependent across time: the observation at timestamp \(t\) is directly conditioned on observations at \(t-1, t-2, \dots\). Shuffling the rows completely destroys the temporal structure, eliminating the predictive signal.

1.2 Cross-Sectional vs. Time Series (Data Leakage)

Dimension Cross-Sectional Data Time Series Data
Index Arbitrary Row ID Strictly ordered timestamp \(t\)
Independence Independent (I.I.D.) Autocorrelated (Current row depends on prior rows)
Validation Strategy Standard K-Fold Cross Validation Rolling-window / Forward-chaining temporal splits
Why Standard K-Fold Cross Validation Fails on Time Series

If you randomly split a time series into \(K\) folds, the training set will contain data points from 2026 to predict test points from 2024. This violates the arrow of time and introduces catastrophic look-ahead data leakage. Validation must strictly respect temporal causality (train on past, validate on future).

1.3 Decomposition: Trend, Seasonality & Noise

Any real-world time series graph (such as university campus power consumption or daily stock trading volume) can be decomposed into three distinct constituent signals:

  1. Trend (\(T_t\)): The long-term underlying direction of the data over extended periods (upward, downward, or stationary).
  2. Seasonality (\(S_t\)): Short-term, repeating periodic cycles tied to a calendar interval (e.g., afternoon electricity peaks, weekend dips, monsoon umbrella sales).
  3. Noise / Residual Shocks (\(\varepsilon_t\)): Unpredictable, random variations that cannot be explained by trend or seasonality (e.g., unexpected factory shutdowns, viral social media bursts).
The Goal of Time Series Modeling

A forecasting model aims to extract all systematic rules governing Trend and Seasonality. If the model's residuals look like pure, structureless white noise (zero mean, constant variance, no autocorrelation), the model has successfully extracted all available predictive information from the series.

02

Stationarity & Differencing

2.1 The Three Stationarity Criteria

A time series is defined as Weakly (Wide-Sense) Stationary if its statistical properties do not change as time advances. Specifically, it must satisfy three mathematical conditions:

Mathematical Conditions for Stationarity
  1. Constant Mean: \(\mathbb{E}[Y_t] = \mu\) for all \(t\). The series fluctuates around a horizontal baseline rather than drifting upwards or downwards.
  2. Constant Variance: \(\text{Var}(Y_t) = \sigma^2\) for all \(t\). The amplitude of swings remains steady rather than fanning outward in a megaphone shape.
  3. Time-Invariant Autocovariance: \(\text{Cov}(Y_t, Y_{t-k}) = \gamma_k\). The covariance between two points depends solely on their distance / lag \(k\), not on the specific timestamp \(t\).

2.2 Why Machine Learning Fails on Non-Stationary Data

Machine learning models are pattern-matching engines that assume patterns learned on past data remain valid for future data. If a startup's revenue grows by 20% every year, the mean of the historical 10-year data will significantly underestimate tomorrow's baseline. Non-stationary data forces models to extrapolate into unseen distribution regimes, leading to severe generalization breakdown.

2.3 Lag Differencing (\(\Delta Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-1}\))

To eliminate trend from non-stationary data, we apply Lag Differencing. Instead of modeling the absolute level, we model the change from one period to the next:

$$\Delta Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-1}$$

Consider stock prices on 5 consecutive days: \([100, 105, 110, 115, 122]\). While the raw series shows a strong upward trend, the differenced series:

$$\Delta Y = [-, 5, 5, 5, 7]$$

fluctuates closely around a constant mean (\(\approx 5.5\)), converting a non-stationary trending series into a stationary series ready for regression and AR modeling.

03

Lags & Auto Regression (AR)

3.1 Defining Lags as Feature Inputs

In standard supervised learning, we predict target \(Y\) from independent features \(X_1, X_2\). In univariate time series, we have only one column: historical values of \(Y\). We engineer features by creating lags:

  • \(y_t\): Current observation (Today's value to predict).
  • \(y_{t-1}\): Lag 1 (Yesterday's value).
  • \(y_{t-2}\): Lag 2 (Value from two days ago).
  • \(y_{t-k}\): Lag \(k\) (Value from \(k\) time intervals in the past).

3.2 The AR(\(p\)) Model Mathematical Formulation

An Autoregressive model of order \(p\), denoted \(\text{AR}(p)\), models the current value as a linear combination of its past \(p\) lags plus an unpredictable white noise shock:

$$y_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \phi_2 y_{t-2} + \dots + \phi_p y_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t$$

Where:

  • \(c\): Baseline constant intercept related to the series mean.
  • \(\phi_1, \dots, \phi_p\): Autoregressive coefficients (weights learned during training).
  • \(p\): Order of the model (number of past lags included).
  • \(\varepsilon_t\): White noise residual (\(\varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\)).
Example: An AR(2) Temperature Model
$$y_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \phi_2 y_{t-2} + \varepsilon_t$$

Predicts today's temperature using only yesterday's and the day-before-yesterday's temperatures. If \(\phi_1 = 0.6\) and \(\phi_2 = 0.3\), positive momentum carries forward: warm days are followed by warm days.

3.3 Momentum Principle & The Order Selection Dilemma

The AR model operates on momentum. However, choosing \(p\) presents a classic bias-variance dilemma: setting \(p\) too small ignores real historical dependencies (underfitting), while setting \(p\) too large (e.g., \(p = 365\)) dramatically inflates parameters and overfits to historical noise. We need an objective metric to identify which lags carry true signal.

04

ACF vs. PACF & Order Selection

4.1 Autocorrelation Function (ACF) Mechanics

The Autocorrelation Function (ACF) at lag \(k\) measures the linear correlation between observations separated by \(k\) time steps:

$$\rho_k = \frac{\sum_{t=k+1}^N (Y_t - \bar{Y})(Y_{t-k} - \bar{Y})}{\sum_{t=1}^N (Y_t - \bar{Y})^2}$$
  • \(\rho_k \approx +1\): Strong positive correlation (trend momentum).
  • \(\rho_k \approx -1\): Strong negative correlation (mean-reverting oscillation).
  • \(\rho_k \approx 0\): No linear relationship at lag \(k\).

4.2 The Ripple Effect Problem (Indirect Correlations)

Suppose today's temperature is strongly correlated with yesterday's (\(y_t \sim y_{t-1}\)), and yesterday's was strongly correlated with the day before (\(y_{t-1} \sim y_{t-2}\)). Even if \(y_{t-2}\) has no direct causal impact on \(y_t\), the standard ACF will show a high correlation between \(y_t\) and \(y_{t-2}\) due to the indirect "ripple effect" passing through \(y_{t-1}\). Therefore, ACF exhibits a slow exponential decay in AR processes, making it unsuitable for isolating the true order \(p\).

4.3 Partial Autocorrelation (PACF) & Selecting \(p\)

The Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF) measures the pure, direct correlation between \(y_t\) and \(y_{t-k}\) after completely controlling for and removing the linear effects of all intermediate lags \(y_{t-1}, \dots, y_{t-(k-1)}\).

How to Use PACF to Choose AR Order \(p\)
  1. Plot the PACF with shaded 95% confidence bands (\(\pm \frac{1.96}{\sqrt{N}}\)).
  2. Observe where the bars cut off abruptly: in an AR(\(p\)) process, the PACF has significant spikes up to lag \(p\) and cuts off sharply to near zero thereafter.
  3. If only Lag 1 and Lag 2 cross the significance band, set \(p = 2\) and estimate an \(\text{AR}(2)\) model.
05

Random Shocks & Moving Average (MA)

5.1 Random Shocks / Innovations (\(\varepsilon_t\))

Real-world systems are regularly buffeted by unpredictable external events (e.g., sudden government regulatory announcements, weather storms, unexpected equipment failures). In time series analysis, these unexpected events are formalized as Random Shocks (Innovations):

$$\varepsilon_t = \text{Actual Value} - \text{Predicted Value}$$

These shocks bump the time series off its expected path.

5.2 Moving Average MA(\(q\)) Model Structure

Instead of predicting \(y_t\) from past series values (as in AR), the Moving Average model of order \(q\), denoted \(\text{MA}(q)\), predicts \(y_t\) as a weighted sum of past forecast errors (shocks):

$$y_t = c + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1 \varepsilon_{t-1} + \theta_2 \varepsilon_{t-2} + \dots + \theta_q \varepsilon_{t-q}$$
Important Distinction: MA Model vs. Rolling Mean

An \(\text{MA}(q)\) model is NOT a rolling average of past prices (like a 50-day moving average). It is a regression on past unobserved error shocks (\(\varepsilon_{t-j}\)), capturing how quickly an unexpected disturbance dissipates.

5.3 AR vs. MA Model Comparison Matrix

Feature Auto Regressive: AR(\(p\)) Moving Average: MA(\(q\))
Input Predictors Past values of the series (\(y_{t-1}, \dots, y_{t-p}\)) Past random shocks / errors (\(\varepsilon_{t-1}, \dots, \varepsilon_{t-q}\))
Core Assumption Driven by historical SIGNAL (Momentum) Driven by historical NOISE (Shock Absorption)
Intuition If values were high yesterday, they stay high today A shock bumps the series, and the effect decays after \(q\) steps
Diagnostic Tool PACF cuts off after lag \(p\); ACF decays slowly ACF cuts off after lag \(q\); PACF decays slowly
